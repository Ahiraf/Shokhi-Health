## Start with a trusted person
A girl or woman does not have to solve a difficult health or safety problem alone. A trusted family member, teacher, community health worker, social worker, or service provider can help her understand choices and reach care. Listening should be respectful and private where possible. No one should be blamed for asking a question or seeking help.

## Adolescent-friendly information
Adolescents need age-appropriate information about their bodies, relationships, safety, nutrition, mental wellbeing, and services. Information should use simple words, allow questions, and respect the young person's privacy. A girl can ask a health worker to explain a service in a way she understands.

## Keeping girls in school
Poverty, disability, disasters, child labour, child marriage, and unsafe journeys can interrupt education. Families, schools, and communities can help girls continue learning by reducing barriers, offering practical support, and welcoming a safe return after absence. Education supports health, confidence, and future choices.

## Digital safety
Digital learning can help children and adolescents reach information, but online spaces can also expose them to harassment, pressure, or harmful requests. Keep personal details private, use a trusted adult or teacher when something feels unsafe, and save evidence of serious threats if it is safe to do so. Immediate danger needs local emergency help.

## Child marriage and health
Child marriage can limit education, choices, and access to health care, and can expose a girl to health and safety risks. Prevention should support girls' agency, education, families, and communities. A girl facing pressure should be supported without blame and connected with a trained protection or health service.

## Responding when a child shares harm
When a child or adolescent says someone harmed or threatened them, listen calmly, believe them, and avoid repeated questioning. Do not pressure them to confront an unsafe person. Help them reach a trained protection worker or another safe service. If there is immediate danger, move to safety and seek emergency help.

## Violence is not her fault
Violence, coercion, harassment, and sexual abuse are never the survivor's fault. A person may need medical care, emotional support, protection planning, and confidential referral. Use a safe device and a trusted service when privacy is a concern. Shokhi should not investigate or promise secrecy it cannot guarantee.

## Safe referral
A safe referral explains what kind of service may help, checks whether the person can reach it safely, and respects her decision. The helper should avoid sharing identifying details unnecessarily. If a child is involved, safeguarding procedures and trained local services should guide the next step.

## Birth registration and access
Birth registration helps children establish legal identity and access services. Families who face barriers can ask a local government office, community worker, or child-protection service what documents and steps are needed. Shokhi can explain the general idea but should not invent local fees, deadlines, or document requirements.

## Disability-inclusive care
Children and women with disabilities should be included in health care, education, social protection, and decisions about their own lives. A service may need an accessible entrance, plain language, sign support, communication assistance, extra time, or a companion chosen by the person. Ask what support the person prefers.

## Respectful communication
Do not assume a disabled person cannot understand, decide, or speak for herself. Address her directly, ask before helping, and give information in an accessible format. Family support is valuable, but the person's preferences and privacy still matter.

## Community health workers
Community health workers can connect families with antenatal care, immunisation, nutrition, family planning, protection, and other services. A worker may know which nearby facility provides a particular service. Shokhi should encourage users to confirm current opening times and availability locally.

## School health support
Schools can be safe places to provide health information, emotional support, referrals, and practical help with menstruation or disability. A student who feels unsafe or is being bullied should be connected with a trusted teacher or safeguarding focal person, while protecting her privacy as much as possible.

## Girls' participation
Programs work better when girls and adolescents are listened to and included in decisions affecting them. Ask what she needs, what feels safe, and who she trusts. Do not treat participation as permission to expose private information or make a decision for her.

## Family support without pressure
Families can support a girl by listening, helping her reach care, and respecting her choices. Pressure to marry, have sex, become pregnant, stop school, or hide violence is not health support. When family involvement could increase danger, seek confidential guidance from a trained service.

## Emergency and non-emergency help
Immediate danger, severe injury, heavy bleeding, fainting, or thoughts of self-harm need urgent in-person help. For non-emergency concerns, a qualified health worker, social worker, school counsellor, or protection service can help plan the next step. This educational summary is not a substitute for local assessment.
