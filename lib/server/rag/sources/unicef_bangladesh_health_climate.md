## Nutrition supports everyday life
Good nutrition supports growth, learning, immunity, pregnancy, recovery, and daily energy. Families can choose a varied diet using foods available locally, such as vegetables, fruits, pulses, eggs, fish, or meat. Food needs vary by age, pregnancy, illness, and household resources, so local health advice matters.

## Anaemia and tiredness
Anaemia and other nutrient deficiencies can affect children, adolescents, pregnant women, and mothers. Ongoing tiredness, weakness, dizziness, or breathlessness should be discussed with a qualified health worker because several conditions can cause these symptoms. Do not start high-dose supplements without advice.

## Nutrition during pregnancy
Pregnancy increases the importance of regular antenatal care and practical nutrition support. A health worker can advise on local foods, prescribed supplements, and warning signs. Shokhi can help prepare questions, but it should not prescribe a dose or replace antenatal assessment.

## Adolescent nutrition
Adolescence is a period of rapid growth. Girls need enough food, rest, and support to learn and stay healthy. Menstrual blood loss, food insecurity, infections, and other health concerns can affect energy. Persistent weakness or dizziness deserves a health-worker discussion rather than blame.

## Safe drinking water
Families should follow local government and health guidance about water safety, especially after flooding, salinity, pipe damage, or other disruption. Use a safer source or treatment method recommended locally. If several people become ill after using water, seek public-health or medical advice.

## Handwashing and hygiene
Handwashing with soap or an appropriate local alternative at key times helps reduce the spread of infection. Children need facilities they can reach and understand. Water points should be safe, accessible, and usable by people with disabilities.

## Menstrual dignity
Girls and women need privacy, clean absorbent materials, water, soap, and a safe place to change or wash. Schools, workplaces, shelters, and homes should plan for disposal or washing. Menstruation is normal; teasing, exclusion, and forcing someone to use unsafe materials are harmful.

## WASH in schools
Schools need toilets that are private, safe, accessible, and usable during menstruation. Reliable water and handwashing support health and attendance. A student who cannot access a toilet safely should be able to tell a trusted teacher or school manager without shame.

## Flood and salinity preparedness
Floods, cyclones, salinity, and damaged water points can interrupt food, medicines, menstrual supplies, schooling, and health care. Families can keep a small emergency kit, protect important documents, follow official warnings, and plan how to reach a safe place. Local disaster guidance takes priority.

## Heatwave safety
Children, pregnant women, older people, and people with health conditions may be more vulnerable in extreme heat. Follow local alerts, rest in a cooler place, drink safe water, wear light clothing, and avoid the hottest hours when possible. New severe symptoms need medical attention.

## Climate-resilient health services
Health services and schools need plans for heat, floods, cyclones, and disease risks. Families should check whether a clinic, pharmacy, school, or water point has moved or changed hours after a disaster. Do not assume a service is operating normally during an emergency.

## Mental wellbeing after a disaster
Displacement, loss, fear, interrupted school, and family stress can affect mood and sleep. Listening, routine, play, supportive adults, and connection with services can help. Persistent distress, inability to function, self-harm thoughts, or immediate danger need prompt professional support.

## Safe food during emergencies
After a disaster, follow local advice about safe food and water. Avoid food that is spoiled, contaminated, or stored unsafely. Infants, pregnant people, older adults, and people with chronic conditions may need extra support to maintain safe nutrition.

## Protecting medicines and supplies
An emergency kit can include essential medicines prescribed for the family, menstrual materials, safe water, food, a torch, phone charging support, and important documents. Medicines should be kept dry and used only as directed. Ask a health worker what to do if supplies are lost.

## Access for people with disabilities
Emergency messages, shelters, toilets, water points, and health services should be accessible. Ask the person what communication or mobility support they prefer. Do not separate a person from their chosen support person unless safety procedures require it.

## When to seek care
Severe pain, heavy bleeding, fainting, trouble breathing, confusion, convulsions, severe dehydration, or danger to a child or pregnant person need urgent care. For less urgent concerns, contact a qualified health worker and explain the symptoms, timing, and any recent heatwave, flood, or water disruption.
